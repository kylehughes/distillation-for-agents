# Operators for finding and simplifying structure

Select an operator because it addresses an observed difficulty. Use a few useful operators, then return to the task; do not execute this catalog as a checklist. Keep the required result, available evidence, and acceptable losses in view. Scale the challenge to the consequence of being wrong.

Treat the procedures below as contemporary adaptations of the cited research. Do not attribute the complete procedures to the historical authors or treat their differing philosophical projects as a consensus. Follow source IDs to the bibliography only when provenance or the original argument matters.

## 1. Frame the purpose and preservation requirements

**Use when** the request says “simplify,” “improve,” or “find the essence” without identifying the difficulty, or when a preferred solution has become the definition of the problem.

**Do:** State what someone needs to understand, decide, experience, or accomplish, under which conditions. Identify what the result must retain: answers, capabilities, obligations, distinctions, or expressive qualities. Separate requirements from preferences. Identify which sources establish correctness; do not grant the current artifact that authority automatically. Reframe “we need another dashboard” as the supported difficulty, such as discovering blocked work too late.

**Challenge:** Imagine a beautifully simple result that fails the actual purpose. Identify what it lost. If the purpose remains uncertain, preserve the relevant alternatives or make conditional progress. Avoid inventing a universal essence to fill the gap.

**Basis:** [R01](sources.md#r01), [R33](sources.md#r33), [R44](sources.md#r44), [R50](sources.md#r50).

## 2. Separate observation, interpretation, and uncertainty

**Use when** emotional language, prestige, labels, disputed motives, or confident summaries obscure what the evidence establishes.

**Do:** Record the relevant observation with its source, context, and status. Distinguish the inference drawn from it and the questions still open. Replace promotional or alarming descriptions with inspectable properties. Retain context that changes meaning: sequence, promises, roles, prior patterns, and qualifications. Compare plausible interpretations when the evidence leaves a material ambiguity.

**Challenge:** Check whether the rewrite promotes a report into a confirmed finding, an association into a cause, or an unknown outcome into failure. Also check whether physical redescription discarded meaning or organization. Calling a performance “vibrations” does not settle its value. Do not manufacture an uninterpreted view from nowhere or erase warranted judgments about harm.

**Basis:** [R02](sources.md#r02), [R03](sources.md#r03), [R04](sources.md#r04), [R22](sources.md#r22), [R33](sources.md#r33).

## 3. Select the explanatory question

**Use when** an answer sounds true but does not resolve the inquiry, or different participants appear to answer different questions.

**Do:** Determine whether the task asks for a definition, material composition, organization, cause, function, invariant, or distinction relevant to action. Separate how an arrangement originated from its intended purpose, actual effects, and justification for continuing it. Use more than one account when the question requires it. Inspect relationships as well as ingredients.

**Challenge:** Ask whether the answer merely changes the subject. Identifying what a device is made from does not explain why it failed. Naming a procedure's historical purpose does not establish that it still serves that purpose. Preserve a philosophical inquiry into essence as such when requested; do not silently substitute task usefulness for metaphysical necessity.

**Basis:** [R09](sources.md#r09), [R10](sources.md#r10), [R11](sources.md#r11), [R52](sources.md#r52).

## 4. Test necessity and sufficiency separately

**Use when** defining a concept, writing eligibility rules, designing categories, or deciding which features constitute a kind.

**Do:** Collect ordinary instances, clear noninstances, and a difficult boundary case. Propose the definition after seeing some variation. Test necessity by seeking a genuine instance without the proposed feature. Test sufficiency by seeking a noninstance with every proposed feature. Correct the definition or expose its intended scope when a counterexample succeeds. Enumerate a finite case space when doing so is practical and relevant.

**Challenge:** Do not treat a familiar example as the definition, or a commonly observed feature as a necessary one. Check whether an expanding chain of exceptions protects the first category instead of improving it. Consider explicit subtypes or overlapping similarities when a common core is unsupported. Do not infer universal adequacy from a few passing examples.

**Basis:** [R07](sources.md#r07), [R09](sources.md#r09), [R29](sources.md#r29).

## 5. Vary the case to find candidate invariants

**Use when** the current implementation, embodiment, or familiar example may be mistaken for a requirement.

**Do:** Vary material, medium, scale, timing, ownership, implementation, or environment. Start with one relevant change, then consider interacting changes when warranted. Ask what can change while the required identity, function, or guarantee survives. Identify where the proposed requirement comes from: the task, evidence, an implementation choice, or familiarity. Seek actual alternatives after imaginative variation.

**Challenge:** Distinguish a candidate invariant from proof of necessity. Failure to imagine an alternative can reflect limited experience. A feature constant in observed examples may reflect selection rather than essence. Test changed conditions before removing redundancy; a component unnecessary during normal operation may be required under failure. State which notion of “the same” the comparison uses.

**Basis:** [R09](sources.md#r09), [R28](sources.md#r28), [R31](sources.md#r31).

## 6. Replace labels with consequences and mechanisms

**Use when** terms such as “reliable,” “fair,” “efficient,” or a technical diagnosis appear to end an explanation prematurely.

**Do:** Specify what would happen under which conditions, what observation would distinguish interpretations, and what interactions produce the result. Temporarily explain the subject without its central label. Restore technical vocabulary once it names an understood relationship. Rewrite vague prose with an identifiable subject, action, condition, and consequence where appropriate.

**Challenge:** Check whether the proposed definition predicts or clarifies anything. “It moved because of energy” may merely rename the question. Preserve normative disagreements when operational meanings differ; do not define a disputed value into one convenient metric. Clarifying a claim's meaning does not establish its truth. Retain necessary qualifications even when they lengthen the sentence.

**Basis:** [R27](sources.md#r27), [R35](sources.md#r35), [R37](sources.md#r37).

## 7. Compare explanations through discriminating evidence

**Use when** a compelling explanation fits existing observations but may not uniquely account for them.

**Do:** Identify a credible alternative when one could change the conclusion. Specify an observation or authorized intervention for which the alternatives predict different results. Examine evidence that the preferred account handles poorly, including measurement, selection, and auxiliary assumptions. Obtain targeted evidence when the distinction matters; otherwise preserve the uncertainty. Avoid inventing numerous equally weighted possibilities merely to appear balanced.

**Challenge:** Distinguish matching known observations from predicting a new consequence, and prediction from identifying a unique cause. A derivation may be valid relative to premises whose empirical adequacy remains uncertain. A surprising observation may expose a measurement problem rather than immediately refute the central theory. Keep the explanation revisable without making it immune to contrary evidence.

**Basis:** [R22](sources.md#r22), [R23](sources.md#r23), [R33](sources.md#r33), [R47](sources.md#r47).

## 8. Collect cases and divide where consequences change

**Use when** many labels conceal a common structure, or one shared label conceals materially different cases.

**Do:** Compare cases by the features relevant to the intended question. Combine cases that share the needed explanation or treatment. Split them where a condition changes a required conclusion, response, or obligation. Permit overlapping families or explicit subtypes when a universal definition becomes strained. Use a common diagnostic framework while allowing different results for different circumstances.

**Challenge:** Check both directions: did a merger discard a necessary distinction, and does a retained split contribute anything? Do not infer a single essence from a common noun. Do not treat every case as incomparable simply because details differ. State whether the grouping tracks a supported structure or supplies a useful convention for this use.

**Basis:** [R08](sources.md#r08), [R25](sources.md#r25), [R29](sources.md#r29), [R50](sources.md#r50).

## 9. Decompose along supported boundaries

**Use when** simultaneous concerns or tangled dependencies prevent useful local reasoning.

**Do:** Map relationships before selecting parts. Look for responsibilities, decisions that can vary independently, clusters of strong interaction, and relevant timescales. Study correctness, desirability, and cost in separate passes when that improves attention. Keep cross-boundary assumptions explicit. Choose boundaries that conceal changeable implementation decisions while exposing necessary commitments.

**Challenge:** A convenient chart or file structure is not evidence of independence. Inspect shared resources, feedback, synchronization, and common causes. If nearly every important effect crosses the proposed boundary, enlarge or redraw the unit of analysis. Treat near decomposability as conditional on interaction strengths and timescales. Do not count extra modules or headings as an improvement without a corresponding reduction in reasoning or coordination burden.

**Basis:** [R08](sources.md#r08), [R15](sources.md#r15), [R21](sources.md#r21), [R39](sources.md#r39), [R40](sources.md#r40), [R41](sources.md#r41).

## 10. Reconnect the parts and recover the behavior

**Use after** decomposing an explanation, solution, process, or artifact.

**Do:** Restore the relationships and conditions suspended during local analysis. Examine sequence, timing, shared state, resource contention, thresholds, and feedback as relevant. Trace an important case through the assembled account, including downstream obligations and exceptional conditions. For expressive work, evaluate arrangement and interaction as well as isolated components.

**Challenge:** Verify that local adequacy survives composition. Correct parts can interact incorrectly; individually dispensable details can jointly support an effect. An inventory of ingredients does not recover organization. A solution to each subproblem does not establish that their assumptions are mutually compatible. If the account cannot reproduce the behavior it was meant to explain, restore the missing relationship or revise the decomposition.

**Basis:** [R04](sources.md#r04), [R21](sources.md#r21), [R34](sources.md#r34), [R38](sources.md#r38), [R39](sources.md#r39).

## 11. Change representation

**Use when** the information is necessary but its current form obstructs comparison, inference, or use.

**Do:** Translate prose into a table, timeline, diagram, state description, worked example, or equation when that reveals the relevant relationships. Alternatively, replace an overformal representation with a concrete case. Map the original information into the new form so omissions remain inspectable. Choose a form appropriate to the audience and question rather than maximizing visual or mathematical neatness.

**Challenge:** Check whether distinctions became implicit or disappeared. A timeline can conceal uncertainty about dates; a hierarchy can imply authority the source never established; a number can hide an unresolved value judgment. Ask which required questions are easier to answer and which have become harder. Restore necessary nuance or use complementary representations where one form cannot carry it adequately.

**Basis:** [R34](sources.md#r34), [R37](sources.md#r37), [R44](sources.md#r44).

## 12. Solve a nearby problem and return

**Use when** the original problem resists direct solution or its structure is not yet apparent.

**Do:** Try a smaller instance, special case, boundary case, related solved problem, or temporary relaxation of one condition. Work backward from the desired result to prerequisites when that clarifies dependencies. Record exactly what changed. After solving the auxiliary problem, restore the original conditions and explain which part of the solution transfers and why.

**Challenge:** Do not declare the real problem solved while its hard constraint remains suspended. An analogy can suggest a method without establishing that it applies. A special case can disprove a universal claim but cannot generally prove one. Backward planning identifies needed conditions; it does not show that those conditions are achievable or under the agent's control.

**Basis:** [R21](sources.md#r21), [R34](sources.md#r34).

## 13. Count independent commitments and exceptions

**Use when** choosing between a short rule with many patches and a richer account with wider reach.

**Do:** Identify the explanatory work performed by each assumption, parameter, entity, convention, and exception. Compare the complete account, including unexplained cases and information required to apply it. Prefer a shared principle when it generates the relevant consequences without hiding independent adjustments. Remove a commitment only after checking the explanatory loss.

**Challenge:** Brevity, familiarity, few symbols, and theoretical economy are different properties. A rule tailored to every known case can fit perfectly without generalizing. Keep explicit tradeoffs instead of inventing a universal simplicity score. Use minimum description length as a formal method only when its modeling and coding assumptions are actually specified; otherwise present exception accounting as a heuristic. Economy alone does not establish truth.

**Basis:** [R19](sources.md#r19), [R20](sources.md#r20), [R24](sources.md#r24), [R26](sources.md#r26), [R45](sources.md#r45), [R46](sources.md#r46).

## 14. Match scale and precision to the question

**Use when** a simple pattern may be an average, approximation, limiting case, or artifact of resolution.

**Do:** State the scale, conditions, and precision over which the account is intended to work. Identify which errors could change the required judgment. Use justified ranges or qualitative distinctions when the evidence cannot support exact numbers. Retain finer detail where exceptional cases, thresholds, or individual differences matter.

**Challenge:** Simple aggregate behavior does not establish simple underlying mechanisms. Complex appearances do not establish complex basic rules. An average adequate for describing a trend may fail to support a guarantee about every instance. Do not convert approximation into exactness, or demand precision that the subject and evidence cannot provide. Choose tolerance from the task and evidence, not from whichever simplification is easiest to defend.

**Basis:** [R11](sources.md#r11), [R31](sources.md#r31), [R47](sources.md#r47), [R52](sources.md#r52).

## 15. Map accumulations, feedback, and delay

**Use when** a recurring problem survives repeated local fixes, or behavior depends on interaction over time.

**Do:** Identify what accumulates, what increases or decreases it, where information travels, and where delay changes responses. Examine reinforcing and balancing effects as hypotheses to verify. Ask which rules, incentives, goals, or information boundaries reproduce the pattern. Consider whether a new control intensifies the behavior it addresses.

**Challenge:** A visible bottleneck or conspicuous number may be a symptom. A causal diagram records a model; drawing an arrow does not prove a causal relationship. Check omitted influences and conflicting explanations. Do not mechanically apply a ranked list of intervention points or assume that changing a goal always helps. Select an intervention from the diagnosed mechanism and inspect consequences at the relevant timescale.

**Basis:** [R38](sources.md#r38), [R48](sources.md#r48), [R49](sources.md#r49), [R50](sources.md#r50).

## 16. Remove work only after identifying its functions

**Use when** deleting a step, rule, feature, check, meeting, or commitment may reduce ongoing burden.

**Do:** Identify the intended and actual functions of the candidate for removal. Examine normal operation and relevant failures. Determine whether another mechanism already supplies the valuable function, whether the function is still needed, and who relies on it. Challenge inherited requirements when the task permits; do not label every existing obligation essential. Prefer a limited reversible change when consequences remain uncertain.

**Challenge:** A function can be quiet because its protection works. Distinguish explanatory redundancy from deliberate operational redundancy. Removing a meeting may displace coordination into many messages. Removing a control may reduce a self-perpetuating burden or expose an unmet obligation; examine the case. Do not equate fewer commitments with a universally better life or system.

**Basis:** [R05](sources.md#r05), [R12](sources.md#r12), [R13](sources.md#r13), [R14](sources.md#r14), [R36](sources.md#r36), [R42](sources.md#r42), [R53](sources.md#r53).

## 17. Investigate what constitutes a named whole

**Use when** a noun appears to supply an independent cause, actor, or essence without explaining its supporting relationships.

**Do:** Examine what conditions, conventions, components, and relations make the named phenomenon intelligible. For “the organization decided,” identify the relevant people, rules, information routes, and authority. Distinguish a relation that helps constitute an identity from one that merely affects an otherwise identified object. Retain the useful name once its role is clear.

**Challenge:** Do not assume stripping away relations will always reveal an independent kernel. Do not infer that conventional or dependent things are unreal, powerless, or dispensable. This operator borrows a question about dependence; it does not turn Buddhist accounts of liberation into systems theory or resolve philosophical disagreements about intrinsic nature. Use the analysis to clarify the actual task.

**Basis:** [R09](sources.md#r09), [R16](sources.md#r16), [R17](sources.md#r17), [R18](sources.md#r18).

## 18. Retain the distinctions needed for action

**Use when** merging states, compressing evidence, designing an interface, or replacing a nuanced procedure with a uniform rule.

**Do:** Identify situations requiring different answers, responses, or guarantees. Preserve enough information to distinguish them, or specify how the distinction will be recovered before it is needed. For dynamic work, follow relevant later events: identical immediate actions need not imply interchangeable states. Preserve uncertainty when its resolution affects action.

**Challenge:** Ask whether two cases made indistinguishable can still be handled correctly for every supported use. “Confirmed rejection” and “unknown outcome” may differ when retrying has effects. A current ranking can omit scores for a display but fail a later cumulative calculation. Do not require every detail to remain; retain the distinctions supported by the actual use and relevant conditions.

**Basis:** [R41](sources.md#r41), [R43](sources.md#r43), [R44](sources.md#r44).

## 19. Separate an action from the outcome it influences

**Use when** a plan mistakes a desired result for an executable step or treats outside dependencies as controllable.

**Do:** Identify what the agent or participant can do, what it can influence, and what depends on other actors or events. Convert outcomes into concrete actions and observable conditions for updating the plan. Preserve dependencies and contingencies rather than hiding them inside a confident verb such as “ensure.”

**Challenge:** Preparing a clear argument does not guarantee agreement. Sending a request does not establish acceptance. Distinguishing agency from outcome should focus action while retaining collective possibilities, real external consequences, and obligations. Use this limited planning adaptation without requiring acceptance of the stronger Stoic ethical position. Avoid treating uncertainty as a reason for automatic inaction.

**Basis:** [R06](sources.md#r06), [R33](sources.md#r33), [R49](sources.md#r49).

## 20. Audit displaced burden and set a revision condition

**Use before** accepting a consequential simplification or extending a successful model to a new use.

**Do:** Identify whose work becomes easier, whose becomes harder, and which costs move into memory, manual effort, hidden machinery, maintenance, or error recovery. Compare consequential dimensions directly. Preserve unresolved tradeoffs where no justified weighting exists. State the supported scope, important omissions, and evidence or changes that would require reconsideration. Give omitted knowledge a route back into decisions.

**Challenge:** Intellectual relief and local tidiness do not establish improvement. Check whether the new account still meets its obligations and whether further elaboration is likely to change the result enough to justify its cost. Stop when adequately supported; do not claim global minimality. Prefer learning through reversible action where appropriate, while avoiding new procedures whose overhead exceeds their benefit.

**Basis:** [R30](sources.md#r30), [R32](sources.md#r32), [R47](sources.md#r47), [R49](sources.md#r49), [R51](sources.md#r51).
